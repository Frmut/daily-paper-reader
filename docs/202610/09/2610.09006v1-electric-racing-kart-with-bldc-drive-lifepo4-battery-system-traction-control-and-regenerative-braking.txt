# Abstract 

The aim of this project was to convert a petrol-powered go-kart into an electric kart. The kart was to be efficient and at the same time simple in design. In addition, it should not become too heavy, since additional weight impairs the driving behaviour. Reaching these goals required attention to several aspects. The first challenge was the weight distribution. To solve it, the battery cells were divided into three groups, each housed in its own battery box. In this way, the boxes could be placed at suitable positions on the chassis. The weight of the motor and the electronics also had to be taken into account. For high overall efficiency, a motor with a high rated efficiency and battery cells with low self-discharge were installed. A regenerative brake feeds part of the kinetic energy back into the battery. Simple energy recovery was comparatively easy to implement, since the selected motor controller offers a recuperation function. The planned three-stage recuperation, however, required an additional microcontroller (μC). To increase the range further, the kart is equipped with a traction control system developed specifically for this vehicle. It prevents the rear wheels from spinning during acceleration by reducing the motor torque. Spinning wheels waste energy and do not contribute to acceleration, so the traction control saves energy and reduces tyre wear. Chapter 2 describes the design in detail. Chapter 3 evaluates the calculations and measurements. The main results are as follows. The calculated top speed is approx. 65 km ·h−1 . The target driving time of one hour is missed. With 80 % of the nominal battery energy, approx. 44 min result at 50 % motor load. With rated torque, the drive force at standstill exceeds the transmissible traction force by a factor of 1.7 to 2.0. The kart is therefore traction-limited up to approx. 27 km ·h−1 to 43 km ·h−1 , and traction control is essential. Regenerative braking reaches approx. 0 .45 g. On a winding track, it could cover up to about 30 % of the energy demand. The initial charging measurement quantifies the flat LiFePO 4 voltage plateau at approx. 0.7 mV to 1.8 mV per percent state of charge. During the first test runs, the cold cells showed a voltage sag under load that triggered the low-voltage alarm. The kart has no auxiliary 12 V battery. The entire low-voltage system is supplied from the traction battery through an isolated DC/DC converter. Validation of these results by driving tests is the main open point. iii Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau

# 1 Introduction and Objectives 

This chapter presents the objectives of the project (Section 1.1) and describes its innovative aspects (Section 1.2). 

# 1.1 Objectives 

Electric mobility is currently the subject of broad discussion. In motor racing, electric boost systems have been used for some time to increase the torque for short periods. So far, however, the available battery capacity and the resulting short driving time severely limit their use in racing and in road traffic. Energy storage systems that considerably increase range and efficiency are already available. The objective of the project is to adapt these systems specifically to racing. Limited installation space and the lowest possible weight are the boundary conditions. Specifically, a multi-stage recuperation system (recovery of braking energy) is developed. The recovered energy extends the driving time. For short periods, it can also be used for a higher motor torque and thus for higher driving performance. In Formula 1, comparable systems are used under the name Energy Recovery System (ERS). Due to the high torque of the electric motor, large slip occurs at the drive axle during acceleration. To avoid this waste of energy, a traction control system with control algorithms tailored to this application is developed. As in road vehicles, the system is switched off in the event of a fault to ensure safety. 

# 1.2 Innovation 

The challenge of the project is to achieve a high drive efficiency and a long battery runtime in order to keep up with conventional karts powered by combustion engines. This is achieved by controlled feedback of kinetic energy into the battery and by a traction control system developed in-house. The division of the battery pack into 24 single cells allows an optimal weight distribution. Furthermore, a lightweight chassis from kart racing contributes significantly to weight reduction and permits high lateral accelerations. 

Traction control: The system prevents the drive wheels from spinning under strong accel-eration. Large wheel slip consumes a lot of energy and does not contribute to acceleration. Traction control ensures maximum force transmission in the optimal slip range of the tyres. Speed sensors at the rear axle and at both front wheels measure the wheel speeds. Based 1Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

on these signals, a control loop intervenes in the motor management. A protective function switches the system off in the event of a fault. 

Battery and battery management system: The energy recovered during braking must be stored within a very short time. The selected LiFePO 4 battery technology permits very high charging currents. The use of 24 cells helps to absorb short charging current peaks. Each individual cell is monitored by a battery management system (BMS) tailored to the cell type. The BMS controls the charging and discharging process at the same time. Optionally, it allows visualisation on mobile phones and tablets. 

Energy recovery by three-stage recuperation: During braking, part of the kinetic energy is converted into electrical energy and fed into the battery. To integrate the recuperation as efficiently as possible into normal driving, it is divided into three stages: • Stage 1: Recuperation when the throttle is released. Energy feedback starts without any action by the driver. • Stage 2: At the push of a button on the steering wheel, kinetic energy is recovered and the speed is reduced without using the mechanical brake. • Stage 3: If more braking force is required, the hydraulic brake acts on the rear axle in addition to the motor brake. This gives the maximum deceleration, while part of the energy is still recovered. The innovation of the project lies above all in the combination of traction control, battery management and energy recovery by three-stage recuperation. This combination is expected to result in an electric racing kart that can keep up well with conventional petrol karts. 2Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau

2.2.1 Selection of the Motor 

The aim is a motor type with low weight and high efficiency. The research identified four possible motor types. Electric vehicles mainly use synchronous and asynchronous machines. Smaller vehicles, such as golf carts, more often use DC motors, which are almost exclusively permanent-magnet excited. Brushless DC motors are used when a long service life at moderate power is required. The highest power densities are achieved by permanent-magnet machines with liquid cooling. Initially, a liquid-cooled motor from Enstroj [4] was considered because of its impressive performance data. However, such a drive concept was beyond the scope of a student project. The additional effort and installation space of a liquid cooling system also spoke against this solution. With the revised power requirement of approx. 10 kW, several motor concepts are possible, since this power range can still be realised with air cooling. A far more important aspect is a high operating voltage to keep the currents low. At the beginning of the project, the team had hardly any experience with motors for electric mobility. Table 1 therefore compares the main data of the motor concepts considered. On the basis of this comparison and after technical discussions with the supervisors, the model HPM-10kW was selected. Compared with the DC machine, the asyn-chronous motor, especially with a squirrel-cage rotor, has a considerably simpler and more robust design (Figure 2). It therefore requires little maintenance. A disadvantage is the close link between operating speed and stator frequency [5]. An asynchronous machine requires neither permanent magnets nor electromagnets in the rotor. In mains operation, no starting aid is required. A major disadvantage is the lower power density compared with other motor types. An advantage is the high efficiency at high rated power and the correspondingly low losses.

External-pole machine: In its basic design, it corresponds to a DC machine without commutating poles. The field winding is located on poles in the stator, and the three-phase winding is in the rotor. Since the three-phase power has to be transmitted via slip rings, this design is problematic for high power. 

Internal-pole machine: The three-phase winding is located in the stator and the field winding in the rotor. This design is therefore suitable for high power. High speeds require a low number of pole pairs. In salient-pole machines, the individual poles take up a lot of space on the circumference, so the rotors have a large diameter and are only suitable for low speeds. High speeds are realised with cylindrical rotors. Their outer diameter is relatively small, which limits the centrifugal forces. With permanent-magnet excitation in the rotor, these motors are 5Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

suitable for high speeds and power levels in electric vehicles. A further advantage is the lower moment of inertia of the rotor compared with salient-pole machines. As an industrial drive, the synchronous machine has gained considerable importance through the development of frequency converters. It is now available as a variable-speed drive from servo motors up to the highest power ratings. Examples are a 30 MW blast-furnace blower with synchronous motor and starting converter, and drives for cement mills, conveyor systems and rolling stands up to the megawatt range [5].

The brushless DC (BLDC) motor is similar in design to a synchronous ma-chine with permanent-magnet excita-tion in the rotor. A three-phase wind-ing generates a rotating field that drives the rotor equipped with permanent magnets. In this respect, the motor does not work like a conventional DC machine. To obtain an operating be-haviour like that of a DC machine, con-trol and power electronics are required (Figure 4). In the kart, this task is performed by the motor controller. Permanent-magnet excitation allows high power, and the design, which is almost identical to that of a synchronous machine, allows simple production. Medium to high power densities are possible. A four-quadrant operation can be realised with relatively simple power electronics. An input rectifier is not required, since the battery voltage is applied directly to the DC link. A further advantage over conventional DC motors is freedom from maintenance, since brushes and commutator are omitted. 

2.2.5 Selection of the Battery 

The requirements for the battery are manifold. On the one hand, it should offer the highest possible energy density at low weight. On the other hand, it must be able to supply the required discharge current. Since the cells are located right next to the driver, safety had top priority. Specifically, the battery must not explode or catch fire when overcharged or short-circuited, and it must not overheat under overload. With proper use, overload should not occur, since the battery is operated within its specification. Under extreme conditions, such as racing, it can occur very quickly. A further aspect is the weight distribution on the kart. Large battery blocks are difficult to distribute, whereas many single cells allow the available space to be used optimally. With many single cells, however, a battery management system is required to balance unequal states of charge. 2.2.6 Lead-Acid Battery 

Lead-acid batteries are robust and reliable (Figure 5a). A major disadvantage is the low energy density and the resulting high weight at large capacities. Very high currents can be drawn for short periods. Charging should be done with suitable chargers. With adequate care, high-quality batteries reach a service life of more than eight years. Due to its long use as a starter battery in vehicles, this technology is very mature. Overcharging is less critical than with other battery types. It does, however, cause the electrolyte to gas and produces oxyhydrogen, which can be ignited by a small spark. Fires or explosions caused by short circuits are nevertheless very rare. In unsealed batteries, electrolyte losses can be compensated easily by adding distilled water. A battery consists of several cells connected in series and usually has a voltage of 12 V. In the charged state, each cell has an open-circuit voltage of approx. 2.1 V. Deep discharge damages a lead-acid battery permanently. If a long service life is required, the cells should not be discharged below 25 % state of charge. The state of charge can be estimated relatively easily by measuring the cell voltage. 

2.2.7 LiFePO 4 Technology 

This technology is also known as the lithium iron phosphate battery (Figure 5b). Its main advantages are the high short-term load capacity of the cells and the high cycle life. Owing to the thermally and chemically very stable cathode material (iron phosphate), these batteries are comparatively safe against short circuit and overcharging. However, the cells contain flammable materials. With regard to explosions, lithium iron phosphate cells are safer than other lithium-ion cells. A single deep discharge or overcharge can permanently damage a cell, but does not lead to explosion or fire. A short circuit leads to gas release only after a long time. A sustained short circuit, however, can ignite the cell. A disadvantage is the relatively low cell voltage of approx. 3.2 V, which requires many cells to be connected in series. The use of a BMS is therefore essential. The energy density is lower than that of other lithium-ion technologies, but sufficient for this application. Using the full capacity reduces the service life. Due to the very flat discharge curve, estimating the state of charge from the terminal voltage is difficult. Precise values can only be obtained by measuring the discharge current with a current sensor and calculating the charge drawn. 

2.2.8 Lithium Polymer Technology 

LiPo batteries are mainly used in model making, where low weight is the top priority (Figure 5c). Lithium polymer cells react much more sensitively to overstress than most comparable battery types. Harmful influences are above all overcharging and deep discharge, as well as operation with excessive currents or outside the specified temperature range. Damage or excessive heating results in a considerable fire risk. Special chargers are required, and during discharge the current and the cell temperature must be monitored. An advantage is the slightly higher cell voltage compared with LiFePO 4 cells. With large capacities and high charging currents, however, the fire risk is considerable. In addition, ageing processes can unpredictably cause an internal short circuit. For safety reasons, this technology is not suitable for use in the kart. 7Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau

The charging current is then reduced to the set minimum and the cell voltages are balanced. Charging ends when one cell reaches the maximum voltage or all cells reach the bypass voltage.

# 2.7 Braking System 

The braking system is the most important safety device on the kart. In operation, it must always allow the kart to stop safely. In addition, the braking energy is to be converted back into electrical energy and fed into the battery. 

2.7.1 Concept 

The braking concept comprises not only the race-proven hydraulic brake, but also a regenerative brake. In regenerative braking, the kinetic energy of the kart is converted into electrical energy. For this purpose, the electric drive motor is operated as a generator. In this mode, it absorbs the mechanical power of the drive axle and converts it into electrical energy, which is fed back into the battery. The special feature of the regenerative brake is that it works without wear and that energy is recovered with good efficiency. In this way, the kart can be braked mostly with the motor brake, and the mechanical brake only has to be used for hard braking. A similar system is used in the automotive sector. In electric and hybrid vehicles with regenerative braking, the driver usually does not notice it, since it is applied automatically when the brake pedal is pressed. For light braking, only the regenerative brake is used. For harder braking, the hydraulic brake is applied in addition. This coupling was deliberately not implemented in the kart. The brakes are operated separately in order to demonstrate the effect of the regenerative brake more clearly. 

2.7.2 Regenerative Braking 

In a technical sense, recuperation simply means energy recovery. The regenerative brake was first used in rail vehicles, where the traction motors were switched to generator operation for braking. In the early days of this technology, only about 5 % of the energy used could be recovered, and the braking force was weak and uneven. Later, considerably higher recovery rates of up to 50 % with reliable braking force were achieved with asynchronous motors. The technology was continuously developed further. In the automotive sector, brake energy regeneration was introduced after 2000, for example under the name “EfficientDynamics” (BMW). Today, it is used above all in electric and hybrid vehicles. 19 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

2.7.3 Implementation 

The regenerative brake of the kart works as described above and is divided into three recuperation levels: 

Stage 1 – motor brake: The first recuperation stage starts as soon as the throttle is released. It brakes only slightly, comparable to the engine brake of a vehicle with a combustion engine. Despite the weak braking effect, energy is already recovered. 

Stage 2 – light braking: In the second stage, the kart is actively decelerated by the regenerative brake. This stage is activated by pressing a button on the steering wheel. It is well suited for gentle deceleration, for example before a slight bend. 

Stage 3 – hard braking: In the third stage, the hydraulic brake is used in addition to the regenerative brake. This achieves the maximum deceleration of the kart. With the hydraulic brake, however, braking is no longer wear-free, and only a small part of the energy is recovered. Most of the kinetic energy is converted into heat in the hydraulic brakes. The aim of the different stages is braking that is both safe and recovers energy. As described above, the button for the second stage is located next to the display on the steering wheel. Additional use of the hydraulic brake leads to the third stage. Of all stages, the second one is the most efficient. 

2.7.4 Components 

The most important components are the installed 10 kW BLDC motor and the matching motor controller. Energy recovery is realised by the motor controller, which switches the motor to generator operation at the push of a button and thus generates electrical energy. A further component of the recuperation is the μC. Only the μC makes it possible to start recuperation at the push of a button. 

# 2.8 Safety Systems 

The topic of safety systems was discussed intensively in the project team. There are many ways to protect the driver of a kart. The most important are a good brake, protective clothing, a helmet and a neck support. Initially, a stable headrest was to be designed and firmly attached to the kart. This plan could not be implemented, because under the given time pressure a working kart was defined as the main goal. # 3.2 Energy Demand and Driving Time 

Capacity actually usable. The driving time of 55 min (Section 2.3) is based on the full nominal energy of 4 608 W ·h. The cell manufacturer, however, recommends using only 80 % of the capacity in normal operation. With 3 686 W ·h, the driving time at 50 % motor load (5 kW) drops to approx. 44 min. 

Steady-state demand. A second estimate uses the driving resistance model of Section 2.4 with the vehicle data of Table 12 (Appendix A) and a drive efficiency of 0.9. The assumption of 5 kW average power is therefore about 1.3 times the steady-state demand at 60 km ·h−1 . This margin covers acceleration phases. Whether it is sufficient in racing can only be answered by measurements on the track. 

Sensitivity. Up to 60 km ·h−1 , rolling resistance accounts for 61 % to 86 % of the driving resistance. The rolling resistance coefficient is therefore the most sensitive parameter of the model. The value μR = 0.07 used in the design is a conservative assumption and was not measured. Should the real value be only half as large, the demand at 60 km ·h−1 drops from 62 W ·h·km −1 to 43 W ·h·km −1 , i.e. by approx. 31 % (Figure 19). 

Battery size and vehicle mass. The driving time target of one hour determined the battery size. With 24 cells of 2.3 kg each, the battery weighs approx. 55 kg, i.e. almost 30 % of the total mass of 187 kg, and the kart became noticeably heavy. In the actual use as a demonstration vehicle with short rides, this capacity was never needed. Since the number of cells in series is fixed by the 72 V voltage class of the drive, a lighter battery means cells of smaller capacity. Halving the capacity would save approx. 28 kg or 15 % of the total mass, reduce rolling resistance and inertia accordingly, and still allow approx. 30 min at a constant 60 km ·h−1 with 80 % of the energy. # 3.3 Traction Limit and Need for Traction Control 

The force that the rear tyres can transmit is limited by the static friction coefficient μH = 0.9, the vehicle mass and the share x of the weight that rests on the rear axle: 

Fmax = μH m g x 

The mass budget of the drive calculation gives m = 187 kg including a 75 kg driver. The rear axle load share was not measured. For a kart with rear-mounted motor and battery boxes, a range of 50 % to 60 % is assumed. The traction limit therefore lies between approx. 830 N and 990 N. With the rated torque curve of the motor (60 N ·m at standstill), a gear ratio of 3.3 and a wheel radius of 0.12 m, the drive force at standstill is approx. 1 650 N. This is 1.7 to 2.0 times the traction limit. The drive force remains above the limit up to approx. 27 km ·h−1 (60 % rear axle load) or approx. 43 km ·h−1 (50 % rear axle load), as Figure 20 shows. In this speed range, the acceleration of the kart is limited by traction and not by the motor. Driving practice confirmed this. With the traction control switched off, the rear axle could be made to slide with the throttle at any time, and the kart could be drifted without difficulty. Two consequences follow. First, the calculated acceleration of 8.2 m ·s−2 at standstill (Section 2.4) cannot be transmitted. Taking tyre grip and rolling resistance into account, the realistic launch acceleration is approx. 3.8 to 4.6 m ·s−2 . Second, a driver will inevitably spin the rear wheels at full throttle. Beyond the friction maximum, the transmitted force decreases with increasing slip, and the excess energy is converted into heat and tyre wear [11]. The traction control is therefore a necessary part of the drive concept. This agrees with the estimate made during planning that traction control becomes useful from a torque of approx. 100 N ·m at the rear axle. With 60 N ·m × 3.3 ≈ 200 N ·m, this value is clearly exceeded. 24 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau

# 3.5 Battery Behaviour during Initial Charging 

During initial charging, the 24 cells were connected in parallel and charged with a total current of 25 A, i.e. approx. 1 A per cell (0.017 C). Charging was spread over four days with overnight pauses, and the voltage was read every 2 h until, after 44 h of charging, the current had decreased to 4 A at the end-of-charge voltage of 4.0 V (Figure 7). In total, approx. 1 050 A ·h were charged, i.e. approx. 44 A ·h per cell. 

State of charge and capacity. The voltage alone does not tell at which state of charge the cells started. The voltage curve in the BMS documentation [6] is a schematic illustration of a typical LiFePO 4 cell, not a measured curve of the cells used, and is therefore not suitable for reading off state-of-charge values. For LiFePO 4, the open-circuit voltage is almost constant between approx. 20 % and 90 % state of charge, and it shows a hysteresis between charging and discharging of the order of 10 mV that persists even after long rest periods [13, 14]. After storage, the cells rest on the lower branch of this hysteresis. The rest voltage of 3.27 V at the start is therefore compatible with a wide range of states of charge, roughly 20 % to 40 %. The evaluation is therefore reversed. In total, approx. 44 A ·h per cell were charged. With the rated capacity of 60 A ·h, the cells started at approx. 27 % state of charge. Charging losses shift this value slightly upwards, since not all of the charge is stored. New cells are usually delivered partially charged, so this starting point is plausible, and the measurement is consistent with the rated capacity. It does not, however, determine the capacity independently. An evaluation of the same readings by one of the authors at the time, integrating the current from 3.25 V to 3.5 V, likewise gave approx. 60 A ·h. The state-of-charge line in Figure 7 follows from this charge counting, anchored at 100 % at the end of charging and based on the rated capacity. It shows how the state of charge rises linearly while the voltage stays almost constant. Two further sources of uncertainty concern the measurement itself. The charging current was read from the built-in display of the laboratory power supply instead of a calibrated current clamp, and the readings were noted by hand, so the 2 h interval was kept only approximately. Together, these effects limit the integrated charge to an accuracy of roughly ±10 %. A capacity test by full discharge with calibrated instruments would remove these uncertainties. The measurement quantifies the flat voltage plateau of LiFePO 4. Between 4 h (3.36 V) and 36 h (3.46 V) of charging, i.e. between approx. 34 % and 90 % state of charge according to the charge counting, the cell voltage rose by only 100 mV. This is approx. 1.8 mV per percent state of charge. In the middle of the plateau, between 6 h and 30 h, the rise is only 28 mV for approx. 25 A ·h or 42 % of the capacity, i.e. approx. 0.7 mV per percent. The relaxation of the voltage during the overnight pauses, visible as dips of 7 mV and 12 mV after the first two pauses, is of the same order as this rise. It illustrates the slow open-circuit voltage recovery of LiFePO 4,which lasts for hours [13]. A voltage error of only 10 mV therefore corresponds to about 6 % to 15 % state of charge. In the plateau, a state-of-charge estimation based on the terminal voltage 26 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

is thus unusable, and the hysteresis adds a further uncertainty that depends on the history of the cell [14]. The charge has to be counted by current integration, as the BMS of the kart does [15]. This property of LiFePO 4, a very flat open-circuit voltage curve with pronounced hysteresis, had been described in the literature before the project [13]. In 2016, however, it was hardly reflected in the products available to a student project. 

# 3.6 Cell Temperature and Voltage Sag under Load 

A second observation concerns the behaviour of the cells under load. During the first test runs, the cells were cool, and high discharge currents were drawn during acceleration. The terminal voltage then dropped so far that the low-voltage alarm of the battery management system was triggered, although the state of charge was not low. After the cells had warmed up in operation, the alarm no longer occurred. No current or temperature data were recorded for these events, so the observation is qualitative. The behaviour is explained by the internal resistance of the cells. The voltage drop under load is the product of current and internal resistance, and the internal impedance of lithium-ion cells rises considerably at low temperature, while the available energy decreases [16]. That the charge-transfer resistance dominates this rise had already been shown in 2003 [17]. The datasheet range of −35 to 85 °C (Table 8, Appendix A) states where the cell may be operated. It does not state where the cell delivers its full performance. For a kart, this has three consequences. First, a low-voltage threshold based on the terminal voltage alone confuses a cold cell under load with an empty cell. The threshold has to be compensated for the load current or evaluated with a delay. Second, the cells should be warmed up with moderate load before full power is demanded. Third, the temperature has to be treated as a state variable of the battery and as more than a protection limit. A state-of-charge estimation that also uses an internal resistance model [15] would resolve both problems observed in this project, the flat plateau and the load-dependent voltage sag. 

# 3.7 Low-Voltage Supply without Auxiliary Battery 

The kart has no separate 12 V battery. The entire low-voltage system, i.e. microcontroller, sensors, display, throttle signal, relays and battery management, is supplied from the 76.8 V traction battery through the isolated DC/DC converter TEN 8-7212WI (72 V to 12 V) and a downstream 5 V linear regulator (Appendix D). This saves a second battery with its own charging and maintenance, reduces weight and installation space and avoids a discharged auxiliary battery as a source of failure. As a consequence, the low-voltage system is only live while the traction battery is connected. In the kart, this is acceptable, because the safety-relevant functions, the hydraulic brake and the emergency stop switch, are purely mechanical or electromechanical and do not depend on the low-voltage supply. During storage, however, the emergency stop switch must remain released, so that the battery management system stays supplied and protects the cells against deep discharge (Section 4.7). The galvanic isolation of the converter keeps the traction voltage away from the control electronics and the sensor cabling. 27 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

Voltage fluctuations. During commissioning, a consequence of the missing auxiliary battery was observed. The low-voltage rails showed voltage fluctuations. An auxiliary battery is a low-impedance buffer with a capacity of several ampere-hours. The DC/DC converter, by contrast, is rated at 8 W, i.e. approx. 0.7 A at 12 V, and its output is buffered only by a few microfarads (Appendix D). Every load step, for example the switching of a relay or the display, therefore appears as a dip on the rail. 

Coupling of the sensor signals. The steep edges of the Hall sensor pulses could be measured on practically all lines of the low-voltage system. A fast edge contains spectral components far above the pulse frequency itself. Through the common ground return and the parasitic capacitance and mutual inductance between adjacent lines, such edges couple into every neighbouring line [18]. On the kart, the sensor cables run over the whole vehicle next to the motor and battery cables, and the boards are connected by screw terminals and discrete wiring. 

Why smaller structures help. The interference produced and picked up by a circuit scales with its geometry. The radiated field of a current loop is proportional to the loop area and to the rate of change of the current, and as a common rule of thumb, the inductance of a trace or wire is roughly 1 nH per millimetre, so every long connection between converter, filter capacitor and load turns a current step into a voltage spike L di/ dt [18]. The hand-made boards of the kart use through-hole components, screw terminals with a pitch of 3.81 mm and generous trace lengths, which results in large loops. A compact layout with surface-mounted components, filter capacitors placed directly at the converter pins, a continuous ground plane and short, paired supply and return traces reduces loop area and inductance substantially and would have improved the electromagnetic compatibility of the low-voltage system. Measures with the same aim are bulk capacitance on the 12 V and 5 V rails, twisted or shielded sensor lines with their own return conductor, RC low-pass filters at the sensor inputs of the microcontroller and a common-mode choke at the DC/DC input. These observations were made with an oscilloscope during commissioning, but were not recorded.

# 3.9 Mechanical Reserves of the Drivetrain 

Rear axle. The rear axle was calculated with a motor torque of 100 N ·m, i.e. 330 N ·m at the sprocket. This load case is conservative: the rated torque of the motor is 30 N ·m, and the starting torque of the rated curve is 60 N ·m. The maximum equivalent stress of approx. 110 N ·mm −2 is well below the minimum yield strength of C45 in the normalised condition (305 N ·mm −2 for diameters from 16 mm to 100 mm [19]). The static safety factor is therefore approx. 2.8. A fatigue assessment was not carried out. 

Grip as a torque limit. In traction, the torque at the rear axle is limited by tyre grip. With the traction limit of 830 N to 990 N, only approx. 100 N ·m to 120 N ·m can be supported at the wheels. The design load of 330 N ·m thus covers steady traction loads about three times over. Load peaks from kerbs, chain shocks or the acceleration of the drivetrain inertia are not covered by this estimate. 

Chain drive under torque steps. The chain was dimensioned statically for a peak torque of 100 N ·m (Section 2.5.2). In operation, this proved insufficient for the pinion on the motor shaft. The torque step of the electric motor from standstill destroyed the original pinion completely, and a new pinion and chain had to be made from higher-grade materials. The pinion is the most highly stressed element of the chain drive because it has the smallest pitch diameter and the fewest teeth, so the full chain force of approx. 3.6 kN acts on very few teeth. An electric motor delivers its torque within milliseconds, and the play of the chain lets the drivetrain accelerate freely for a moment before the load is taken up. The pinion therefore sees an impact instead of a steady load. In chain design, such shock loads are covered by an application factor that is considerably higher for a drive with sudden torque steps than for uniform load. Two measures follow: a hardened steel pinion, and a limitation of the torque rate in the firmware. The ramp of the traction control, which raises the power demand by at most one PWM step per millisecond (Table 7), also acts as such a limitation. 

Bearings. The most highly loaded bearing B1 next to the sprocket reaches a nominal life of 4 290 h in the design load case. This is below the target of 20 000 h. For a demonstration 29 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

vehicle with a few dozen operating hours per year, the value is sufficient. In continuous racing use, this bearing position would have to be reviewed.

# 4.2 Handling of the Battery 

The LiFePO 4 cells must be handled with particular care. The cell voltage must not fall below 2.8 V, as the cells are otherwise deeply discharged and damaged. The cells are therefore a potential hazard even when the kart is not in use. The terminals are the greatest source of danger. The positive and negative terminals must never be connected to each other. Otherwise, a very high short-circuit current flows, discharges the cells within a very short time and damages them. In the event of a fault, a safety vent on the top of the cells releases a gas mixture. This gas must not be inhaled or get into the nose or eyes. Swelling, deformation, cracks in the surface and fire can also occur. The risk of explosion is low with this cell chemistry. A damaged cell must not be used any longer. Voltage measurements may only be carried out at the terminals of a cell. Care must be taken not to touch any components of the BMS. In summer, the kart should not be left standing in the sun for long. At temperatures above 85 °C, the cells must no longer be loaded. This temperature can be reached, because the battery boxes are powder-coated black and the heat cannot escape well. Any contact with water or 31 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

other liquids must be avoided. The battery boxes must not be left open unattended, since the cells could become dirty and a person could cause a short circuit. The cells contain toxic substances. They do not belong in household waste but must be disposed of properly. For transport or disposal, the terminals should be covered with insulating tape to avoid an accidental short circuit.

# 5 Conclusion and Outlook 

Within one year, a petrol racing kart was converted into an electric kart with a 10 kW BLDC drive, a 76.8 V LiFePO 4 battery with cell-level monitoring, a three-stage energy recovery and a traction control developed in-house. The kart combines ecological and economic sustainability with use in racing. The evaluation in Chapter 3 condenses the calculations and measurements of the project into the following findings. 

Key findings. 

1. The kart meets five of seven requirements. The calculated top speed is approx. 65 km ·h−1 

(Section 3.1). 2. The driving time target of one hour is missed. At 50 % motor load, 55 min result with the full nominal energy and approx. 44 min with the recommended 80 %. At a constant 60 km ·h−1 , approx. 59 min are possible (Section 3.2). 3. Up to 60 km ·h−1 , rolling resistance causes 61 % to 86 % of the driving resistance. The rolling resistance coefficient is therefore the most sensitive model parameter. Halving it lowers the demand at 60 km ·h−1 by approx. 31 %. 4. With rated torque, the drive force at standstill of approx. 1 650 N exceeds the transmissible traction force by a factor of 1.7 to 2.0. Up to approx. 27 km ·h−1 to 43 km ·h−1 , the kart is limited by traction and not by the motor. Traction control is therefore a necessary part of the drive concept (Section 3.3). 5. The realistic launch acceleration of approx. 3.8 to 4.6 m ·s−2 is set by tyre grip. 6. Regenerative braking alone reaches approx. 0 .45 g at rated torque. Hard braking still needs the hydraulic brake, which confirms the three-stage braking concept (Section 3.4). 7. One braking event from 60 km ·h−1 to 30 km ·h−1 returns approx. 4 W ·h. On a winding track, recuperation could cover up to about 30 % of the energy demand (upper estimate). 8. In the voltage plateau of LiFePO 4, the cell voltage changes by only approx. 0.7 mV to 1.8 mV per percent state of charge, and the voltage relaxes by about 10 mV during a pause. The state of charge can only be determined by current integration. Because of the flat plateau and the hysteresis, the starting state of charge cannot be read from the voltage. With the rated 60 A ·h, the charge of approx. 44 A ·h per cell corresponds to a start at approx. 27 %, which is plausible for new cells. The measurement is consistent with the rated capacity, but does not determine it (Section 3.5). 9. Tyre grip limits the torque at the rear axle in traction to approx. 100 N ·m to 120 N ·m. The axle design load of 330 N ·m is therefore conservative for traction loads. The static safety against yield is approx. 2.8 (Section 3.9). 36 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

10. With cool cells, high discharge currents caused a voltage sag that triggered the low-voltage alarm although the battery was not empty. The internal resistance of LiFePO 4 cells is strongly temperature-dependent, so a voltage-based low-voltage threshold has to be compensated for load and temperature (Section 3.6). 11. The kart has no auxiliary 12 V battery. The entire low-voltage system is supplied from the traction battery through an isolated DC/DC converter (Section 3.7). 12. The battery of approx. 55 kg, sized for the one-hour target, makes up almost 30 % of the vehicle mass. For the real duty cycle of a demonstration vehicle, a battery of half the capacity would have sufficed and would have saved approx. 15 % of the total mass. 13. The torque step of the electric motor from standstill destroyed the original pinion on the motor shaft. A static chain design with 100 N ·m does not cover such shock loads. A hardened pinion and a torque-rate limitation in the firmware are required. 14. Without the buffer of an auxiliary battery, the low-voltage rails showed voltage fluctuations, and the edges of the Hall sensor pulses coupled into the whole low-voltage system. Buffering and a compact, EMC-oriented layout of the DC/DC and filter stage are decisive, because smaller structures mean smaller loop areas and lower inductance. 

Added value of the project. The kart is a fully documented reference design of an electric racing kart in the 10 kW class, from the requirements through calculation and component selection to the firmware. In 2016, it brought lithium iron phosphate cells into a vehicle at a time when this chemistry was still uncommon in European passenger cars. Only from about 2020 did LiFePO 4 gain a large share of the electric vehicle market, and by 2022 it reached just under 30 % [22]. The project encountered two weaknesses of a purely voltage-based battery monitoring, the flat voltage plateau and the load- and temperature-dependent voltage sag. State-of-charge estimation with cell models addresses these problems today [15]. The low-voltage supply without an auxiliary battery is a further simplification that is unusual in vehicle design. The hardware of the traction control can be built with standard components, and its know-how lies in the software. An implementation in other electric vehicles is therefore possible with comparatively little effort. Firmware, schematics, bills of material and calculations are publicly available [10], so that later student teams and other institutions can reproduce and extend the work. As a team, Johannes Stockhammer, Tom Rettenwander and Philipp Huber reached the final of the Austrian youth innovation competition Jugend Innovativ 2015/16 in the special category Sustainability and received a recognition award [23]. Approx. 1300 working hours are documented in the project diaries (Appendix B). The environmental aspect goes beyond energy consumption. Non-exhaust emissions from tyre and brake wear are a relevant source of particulate matter [12]. Regenerative braking reduces the use of the friction brake, and traction control limits wheel spin. Both measures therefore address non-exhaust emissions. For the kart, this effect remains a hypothesis until it is measured. 

Outlook. The most important open point is the validation on the track. The following test programme is proposed: 1. Data logging of wheel speeds, throttle signal, motor current and battery voltage. The microcontroller already evaluates the wheel speeds at 1 kHz. 37 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau 

2. Launch tests from 0 to 40 km ·h−1 with and without traction control. 3. Lap tests at a kart track with measurement of lap time, energy per lap and recovered energy per lap from the current integration of the BMS. 4. A capacity test of the cells by a full discharge at a defined current, to verify the rated 60 A ·h and to obtain the open-circuit voltage curve of the cells actually used. 5. Identification of the model parameters: rolling resistance by a coast-down test, rear axle load by weighing the kart with driver. 6. Tuning of the slip threshold and the ramps of the traction control based on the recorded data. 7. Determination of tyre wear by weighing the tyres before and after a defined distance. 8. Oscilloscope measurements of the 12 V and 5 V rails and of the sensor lines, followed by a revised, compact DC/DC and filter board. 9. Measurement of the chain force during launches with a strain gauge on the motor mount. 10. A smaller and lighter battery sized for the measured duty cycle, combined with fast charging between sessions. LiFePO 4 cells tolerate high charging currents, so short breaks can be used to recharge instead of carrying the energy for a full hour. Since the cells accept charge better when warm, charging directly after driving is favourable. The charger and the BMS limits have to be matched to the higher currents. New technologies that are first used in motor racing often find their way into series production vehicles later on. With the proposed measurements, the kart can make a small but measurable contribution to this. 38 Electric Racing Kart · HTL Braunau
