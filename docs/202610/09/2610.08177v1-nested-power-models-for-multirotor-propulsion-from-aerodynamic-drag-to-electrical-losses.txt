# Nested Power Models for Multirotor Propulsion: From Aerodynamic Drag to Electrical Losses 

Antonio Franchi 1,2, Aaron Saini 1, Ahmed Ali 1, and Chiara Gabellieri 1,

Abstract —Speed-only aerodynamic power models for multirotor propulsion cannot represent acceleration-dependent effects. This work develops a nested sequence of propulsion-power models that starts from aerodynamic power dissipation and progressively introduces a reversible kinetic-energy rate, torque-dependent electromechanical dissipation, and lumped speed-proportional dissipation. The models are identified using one subset of experiments and validated using the other on a motor-drive– propeller unit. Independent estimates of rotational inertia and aerodynamic drag complement predictive validation by assessing whether the models correctly attribute the measured power to reversible kinetic-energy exchange and irreversible dissipation and, within the latter, to aerodynamic and electromechanical losses. The results show that the reversible kinetic-energy rate is necessary but insufficient for accurate dynamic power prediction. Dissipation proportional to the squared motor torque provides the main additional improvement, while speed-proportional dissipation further prevents irreversible losses from being attributed to reversible kinetic-energy exchange. The resulting methodology provides a reusable and experimentally verifiable basis for developing and selecting dynamic propulsion-power models for multirotor systems. 

Index Terms —Aerial robotics, motor–propeller system, electrical power, dynamic identification, torque-dependent loss, rotor inertia, aerodynamic drag. 

I. Introduction The limited onboard energy of multirotor aerial vehicles makes propulsion-power modeling important for endurance estimation, vehicle and propulsion-system design, mission plan-ning, trajectory generation, and control allocation. Flight-level models relate power consumption to vehicle velocity, thrust, climb rate, and flight direction to estimate consumption, range, endurance, and optimal flight speed [1]–[3]. Experimental and data-driven approaches have similarly identified the power or energy consumption associated with complete flight trajectories [4], while design-oriented methods support propulsion-system selection, sizing, and efficiency optimization [5]–[7]. Together, these approaches demonstrate that compact and physically grounded power models can influence both vehicle design and the way aerial tasks are planned and executed. However, their primary objective is generally to predict total power, energy consumption, or propulsion performance. Such descriptions can predict thrust, rotor speed, current, or total electrical power and support component selection, diagnosis, simulation, and model-based control. They do not generally assess how the predicted power is attributed among reversible kinetic-energy exchange, aerodynamic dissipation, and electromechanical dissipation. Dynamic power prediction is especially relevant when rotor speeds vary rapidly. Trajectory shape and acceleration can have a substantial effect on quadrotor energy consumption [10], while multirotor control and experimental platforms increasingly support precise and agile motion [11], [12]. In these conditions, the power required to accelerate the motor–propeller assembly is not described by a steady-state speed-only relation. Rotor acceleration changes the rotational kinetic energy, but it also changes motor torque and current and may therefore affect irreversible electromechanical dissipation. Furthermore, rapid rotor-speed modulation is not limited to fast translational flight. It also arises in aerial physical interaction, where the vehicle may remain nearly stationary with respect to the surrounding air while producing rapidly varying forces and torques against the environment. Aerial manipulators and multidirectional-thrust platforms have been developed for contact inspection, manipulation, and other interaction tasks [13], [14]. Fully actuated and redundantly actuated multirotors can regulate interaction wrenches and vehicle attitude with limited or no translational motion, but doing so relies directly on coordinated and potentially rapid variations of individual rotor speeds [15]–[17]. Related systems employ reconfigurable propulsion or contact mechanisms to improve interaction accuracy [18], while recent omnidirectional aerial-interaction experiments further demonstrate the relevance of rotor-level actuation models during contact-rich operation [19]. More generally, the growing range of multirotor physical-interaction tasks makes actuator dynamics, limits, and energetic behavior increasingly important [20]. Despite these developments, accurate total-power prediction does not establish correct attribution among the underlying physical contributions. During accelerated rotor motion, re-versible kinetic-energy exchange, aerodynamic dissipation, and torque-dependent electromechanical losses may have correlated effects over a finite operating domain. A model may therefore reproduce the measured electrical power while assigning irreversible loss to the reversible kinetic-energy rate or compensating an inaccurate aerodynamic contribution through an electromechanical-loss term. Such confounding becomes problematic when the model must determine not only the energy required by a trajectory, but also how much is temporarily arXiv:2610.08177v1 [cs.RO] 6 Oct 2026 2

stored, how much is irreversibly lost, and which propulsion component should be improved. This work addresses this gap by developing and experimen-tally assessing propulsion-power models with progressively richer energetic decompositions. The models are identified on one group of complete dynamic experiments and evaluated on separate experiments excluded from identification. Held-out predictive validation is complemented by independent estimates of rotational inertia and aerodynamic drag to assess whether the measured power is attributed consistently among reversible kinetic-energy exchange, aerodynamic dissipation, and electromechanical dissipation. The main contribution of this work is a physically moti-vated sequence of multirotor propulsion-power models that starts from aerodynamic power dissipation and progressively introduces the reversible kinetic-energy rate, torque-dependent electromechanical dissipation, and lumped speed-proportional dissipation. Experiments show that the kinetic-energy rate is necessary but insufficient during accelerated rotor motion, that torque-dependent dissipation provides the principal additional predictive improvement, and that speed-proportional dissipation further improves power attribution and the joint recovery of inertia and aerodynamic drag. This contribution is supported by: 1) a dynamic identification methodology based on realizable speed–acceleration trajectories and validation on complete experiments conducted at nominal acceleration rates ex-cluded from identification; and 2) a validation framework combining held-out predictive accuracy with independent inertia and aerodynamic-drag estimates to assess the attribution of measured power among reversible kinetic-energy exchange, aerodynamic dissipation, and electromechanical dissipation. Related Work Power and energy models for aerial vehicles differ substan-tially in physical resolution and intended use. Component-level models describe propeller loads, motor torque, current, voltage, conversion losses, and battery behavior. Vehicle-level models aggregate propulsion, airframe aerodynamics, and flight dynamics to estimate range, endurance, or mission energy. Planning and control methods then use these models as objectives or constraints for trajectory generation, mission scheduling, and control allocation. Because rotary-wing, fixed-wing, flapping-wing, convertible, and physically interactive aircraft emphasize different energetic mechanisms, this section follows the modeling chain from propulsion fundamentals to vehicle- and mission-level optimization, then positions the rotor-level attribution problem addressed in this work. 

A. Rotor aerodynamics and speed-only power models 

The cubic dependence of rotor power on angular speed origi-nates from standard rotor and propeller aerodynamics. For fixed geometry and operating conditions, thrust and resisting torque are commonly approximated as quadratic functions of rotor speed, so mechanical power scales cubically. Low-Reynolds-number propeller measurements and blade-element–momentum models show, however, that thrust and power coefficients depend on propeller geometry, inflow, Reynolds number, and scale effects [21], [22]. Multirotor models consequently range from constant-coefficient quadratic thrust and drag-torque laws used in dynamics and control [23] to rotor-power-coefficient models embedded in flight simulation [24]. When the aerodynamic coefficients are treated as constant, these actuator laws induce the speed-only cubic power model. This representation has supported aerodynamic-power control at the propulsion-unit level [25], system-level power-consumption models [1], and efficiency analysis of morphing multirotors [26]. Such models compactly represent aerodynamic dissipation, but speed alone cannot express the reversible kinetic-energy exchange and the additional losses associated with rotor acceleration that are central to the present work. 

B. Electric propulsion-chain modeling and design 

A second line of work models the propulsion chain by interconnecting the power source, electronic speed controller, motor, shaft, and propeller. Analytical design methods combine propeller theory, motor constants, current and speed limits, battery capacity, and vehicle requirements to select components and estimate performance [27], [28]. Broader sizing correlations extend this reasoning across electric and fuel-powered UAV categories [29], while multicopter-specific optimization selects motor, propeller, and battery combinations for efficiency [5]. Experimental characterization and sizing studies further address large or heavy-lift multirotors whose propulsion components operate far from the small-quadrotor regime [6]. Component-resolved dynamic models have been developed for both fixed-wing and multirotor propulsion. Fixed-wing for-mulations separately represent the electrical source, controller, motor, and propeller to predict thrust, rotor speed, and current [8]; related dynamic models describe the coupled electric motor and propeller over transient operation [9]. More recent physics-informed approaches combine dimensional analysis, propeller data, test-rig measurements, and torque-based current prediction to improve cross-configuration endurance estimation [30]. These approaches increase propulsion-model fidelity, whereas the present study asks which compact acceleration-dependent terms are identifiable from source-side power and whether the resulting decomposition is physically consistent. 

C. Battery, range, endurance, and vehicle-level power 

Battery-aware aircraft models connect propulsion demand to stored electrical energy, voltage variation, discharge rate, and 3

degradation. Classical electric-aircraft analyses derive range and endurance while accounting for battery-discharge behavior [31]; subsequent work examines how battery degradation changes optimal endurance and range over the aircraft life cycle [32]. For multirotors, practical endurance estimators combine hover and forward-flight requirements with battery and payload information [33], while integrated aerodynamic, motor, and gray-box battery models estimate range, endurance, and optimal speed across multicopter designs [2]. Alternative architectures seek endurance gains through hybrid power systems [7] or solar augmentation at MAV scale [34]. At vehicle level, empirical models fit power or energy directly from flight measurements and operating conditions. Examples include comprehensive battery-based energy models for UAV maneuvers [35], quadrotor energy models identified from exper-imental voltage data [4], and comparisons of drone and ground-vehicle delivery energy [36]. Analytical multirotor models instead express power as a function of thrust, forward velocity, climb rate, flight direction, and aerodynamic contributions [3]. Reviews of drone-energy factors and models show that the appropriate abstraction depends strongly on whether the objective is component design, flight-time estimation, mission planning, or energy management [37]. Across these approaches, accurate total-energy prediction does not by itself establish whether transient power is attributed correctly among stored energy and individual loss mechanisms. 

D. Energy-aware trajectory, allocation, and mission planning 

Power models become decision criteria when trajectories, actuator commands, or missions are optimized. Polynomial-trajectory studies show that kinematic choices can substantially change quadrotor energy consumption [10]. Integrated physical models can be embedded directly in trajectory optimization and feedback control, yielding experimentally verified reductions in consumed energy [38]. Coverage-planning methods use experimentally derived energy models together with sensing-resolution and return-safety constraints [39], while multi-UAV planners jointly optimize coverage assignment and flight speed under battery constraints [40]. Recent logistics planners further combine multirotor dynamics, obstacle avoidance, mission duration, payload, and energy-efficiency objectives [41]. Communication and deployment studies use rotary-wing propulsion models to optimize trajectory and service time jointly with communication energy [42]. Comparative mission simulators extend this perspective to both rotary-wing and fixed-wing vehicles [43]. Within redundant actuation, energy-optimal allocation selects among multiple actuator combinations that realize the same body wrench [17]. These applications make model attribution consequential: if a transient reversible term is treated as dissipation, or an irreversible loss is hidden in an inertial coefficient, the optimization may select a different trajectory, speed, or actuator evolution than a physically resolved model would. 

E. Fixed-wing, flapping-wing, and convertible MAVs 

Fixed-wing and flapping-wing MAVs emphasize energetic mechanisms that differ from hovering multirotors. Long-endurance fixed-wing MAV design couples aerodynamic effi-ciency, lightweight construction, propulsion selection, speed, and stored energy [44]. The program in [45] integrates aerodynamic, mechanical, sensing, and autonomy constraints in flapping-wing systems, while subsequent robotic-flapper experiments reveal unsteady aerodynamic coupling during rapid maneuvers [46]. These platforms reinforce the broader need to separate actuator-energy storage, aerodynamic force production, and irreversible conversion losses even though their actuation principles differ from those considered here. Convertible aircraft combine multirotor-like vertical flight with wing-borne cruise. Tailsitter control spans hover, transition, and forward flight despite strongly changing and imperfectly known aerodynamics [47]; tilt-rotor VTOL systems add con-figuration redundancy and dynamic allocation under actuator failures [48]. Reconfigurable and morphing multirotors likewise use additional actuation to change the relation between rotor thrust and body wrench [18]. At the vehicle-design level, a fully actuated tilting-propeller quadrotor demonstrates the modeling and control implications of redundant actuation [49]. These systems motivate energetic models that remain meaningful when rotor states change rapidly even if the vehicle motion changes comparatively little. 

F. Aerodynamic interaction and physically interactive flight 

The surrounding flow can change rotor thrust, resisting torque, and efficiency. Ground-effect experiments characterize both complete and partial ground interaction in multirotors [50], while obstacle-proximity studies map aerodynamic effects near surfaces and infrastructure [51]. Rotor–rotor interference, vehicle-body interaction, payload aerodynamics, and wake re-circulation create further deviations from isolated-rotor models. Such effects are especially relevant in aerial manipulation and inspection, where the vehicle operates close to the environment and may sustain interaction forces with limited translational motion. The aerial-manipulation literature ranges from early system [13] to broad assessments of the field’s development and open challenges [14], [20]. Fully actuated and redundantly actuated platforms support six-dimensional interaction and contact-force regulation [15], [16], while recent reconfigurable and omnidirectional systems extend these capabilities [19]. Control surveys and shared experimental infrastructures have helped standardize multirotor modeling, control, and reproducible deployment [11], [12]. In these interaction regimes, rotor acceleration and source power can vary substantially even when vehicle velocity is small, making rotor-level transient-power models particularly relevant. 

G. Position of the present work 

The reviewed literature provides models for propeller aero-dynamics, electric propulsion, battery discharge, vehicle-level consumption, endurance, trajectory optimization, convertible flight, redundant allocation, and physical interaction. These models have enabled increasingly accurate prediction and optimization across diverse aerial platforms. Their principal 4

outputs are typically thrust, current, total power, range, en-durance, or mission energy. Comparatively less attention has been given to experimentally determining which acceleration-dependent terms are required to attribute source-side electrical power consistently among reversible kinetic-energy exchange, aerodynamic dissipation, and electromechanical dissipation. The present work addresses this attribution problem through a nested model sequence, held-out dynamic experiments, and independent aeromechanical reference estimates. III. Model-Adequacy Criteria and Methodology for Dynamic Identification and Validation The models introduced in Section III form a nested hierarchy, since each model adds one contribution to its predecessor. Because the preceding model is recovered by setting the additional coefficient to zero, the minimum fitting error cannot increase when the same data and criterion are used. The relevant question is therefore not whether a richer model fits better, but whether its additional complexity is justified by the requirements of the intended task. In addition to predictive accuracy, model adequacy depends on faithfully attributing the predicted power. This requires dis-tinguishing reversible kinetic-energy exchange from irreversible dissipation and, within the latter, aerodynamic from electrome-chanical losses. Separating reversible from dissipated power clarifies how much energy is temporarily stored in rotor motion and how much is irreversibly lost, supporting transient-energy, efficiency, and thermal assessments. Separating aerodynamic from electromechanical dissipation further indicates whether efficiency improvements should primarily target the propeller aerodynamics or the motor-drive system. With reference to the physically motivated models introduced in Section III and to a propeller representative of multirotor robotic platforms, this work addresses the following questions: 1) Is the speed-only aerodynamic model 𝑃 1 sufficiently accu-rate for estimating power during accelerated rotor motion? 2) If acceleration dependence is required, is the reversible kinetic-energy rate introduced by 𝑃 2 sufficient, must the acceleration-producing torque also enter the torque-dependent electromechanical loss represented by 𝑃 3, or is the speed-proportional dissipation represented by 𝑃 4 also required? 3) How well can the dynamic models 𝑃 2–𝑃 4 separate reversible from dissipated power and, for 𝑃 3 and 𝑃 4, aerodynamic from electromechanical dissipation? To address these questions, the models are evaluated in terms of both predictive accuracy and physical power attribution. Predictive accuracy is evaluated on complete experiments excluded from parameter identification. Physical attribution is assessed by comparing the identified values of 𝐽 and 𝑐 𝜏 

with independent aeromechanical estimates. These comparisons indicate whether the models assign the measured power consis-tently to inertial energy exchange, aerodynamic dissipation, and electromechanical dissipation, rather than merely predicting their sum accurately.

A. Scope and limitations 

The experiments cover positive rotor frequencies from 60.28 to 195.05 Hz and reconstructed frequency rates from 

−1489 .83 to 2093.45 Hz/s. The conclusions therefore concern interpolation within this experimental domain and the particular motor-drive–propeller unit considered. The reported MAPE values apply only to measured source-side powers satisfying 

𝑃 ≥ 20 W. The independent values of 𝐽 and 𝑐 𝜏 are point estimates obtained from segmented inertia determination and force– torque measurements, respectively. Consequently, the reported parameter discrepancies quantify agreement with these ref-erence values but do not establish statistical compatibility. The interpretation of parameter recovery is therefore limited to the relative proximity of the identified and independently determined point estimates. The remaining acceleration-organized residuals indicate that the four considered contributions do not describe all power variations. Possible unmodeled effects include braking and DC-bus energy return, temperature dependence, electronic-speed-12 

controller switching and conduction losses, iron losses, voltage dependence, and residual synchronization or differentiation errors. Moreover, 𝑘 tl and 𝑞 1 are lumped coefficients over the investigated operating domain and should not be interpreted as isolated microscopic loss parameters. 

B. Implications and conclusion 

Speed-only models may remain adequate near steady oper-ation but can become strongly inaccurate during rapid rotor-speed modulation. Models 𝑃 3 and 𝑃 4 remain compact enough for energy-aware trajectory generation, control allocation, and propulsion-system analysis while representing both reversible kinetic-energy exchange and acceleration-dependent dissipation. This distinction clarifies how much energy is stored temporarily in rotor motion and how much is lost through aerodynamic and electromechanical dissipation, supporting transient-energy esti-mation, efficiency assessment, and interpretation of identified propulsion parameters. This work developed and experimentally evaluated a nested sequence of propulsion-power models progressing from aerody-namic power dissipation to reversible kinetic-energy exchange, torque-dependent electromechanical dissipation, and lumped speed-proportional dissipation. Held-out prediction establishes that the kinetic-energy rate is necessary but insufficient and that torque-dependent dissipation provides the principal additional improvement. The speed-proportional contribution further im-proves prediction and provides the closest joint recovery of the independently determined inertia and aerodynamic coefficient. Thus, held-out prediction and independent aeromechanical ref-erences assess complementary properties and together provide a physically grounded basis for selecting dynamic propulsion-power models.
