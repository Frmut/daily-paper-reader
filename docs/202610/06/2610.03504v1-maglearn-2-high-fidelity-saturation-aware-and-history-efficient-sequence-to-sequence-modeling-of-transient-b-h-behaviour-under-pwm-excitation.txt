Abstract—Accurate transient B–H modeling under pulse-
width-modulated (PWM) excitation is challenging since the
magnetic-field response depends on both the instantaneous flux
density and the preceding magnetisation trajectory. Although
sequence-to-sequence models enable waveform prediction, exist-
ing studies have not jointly addressed how much magnetic history
is required, how saturation region should be handled, and how
trained models can be adapted to unseen materials. To address
these gaps, this paper presents MagLearn 2, a condition-aware,
short-window LSTM-based sequence-to-sequence framework for
reconstructing the future H(t) response from the historical
B(t) and H(t) trajectories. A saturation-aware auxiliary module
selectively replaces saturation-dominated predictions within the
complete waveform forecast. In a representative saturated 3C90
case, the auxiliary module reduces the sequence-level relative
RMSE from 67.84% to 9.19%. To understand how much the
history samples contributes to the predictions, an occlusion-based
sensitivity analysis is conducted to quantifies the contribution
of magnetic history and reveals that that the information most
relevant to prediction is concentrated near the forecast boundary.
In the independent evaluation for MagNet Challenge II, the
proposed framework’s best-performing model achieved the lowest
reported average RMSE to date on the sequence-prediction task,
at 2.2%.

I. INTRODUCTION
Advances in semiconductor devices have enabled higher
switching frequencies and power densities in power electronic
converter designs, while imposing increasingly demanding
requirements on magnetic components [1]. These advances are
particularly important for data-centre power supplies requiring
fast dynamic response under rapidly changing computational
loads [2]. However, as semiconductor switching performance
improves, magnetic component remain major contributors to
converter volume, power loss, and thermal constraints. Their
core and winding losses, temperature rise, and nonlinear
dynamic behaviour can therefore limit further improvements

in converter efficiency, reliability, and control performance
[3]. Conventional modeling methods of magnetics in power
electronics has primarily focused on steady state, where B–H
behaviour is characterised over complete excitation cycles.
However, transient operation requires the instantaneous mag-
netic response to be determined from only the available
preceding trajectory. Under pulse-width-modulated (PWM)
excitation, the flux-density waveform B(t) generally contains
switching-frequency components superimposed on a lower-
frequency envelope, producing nested and potentially unclosed
minor loops in the B–H trajectory [4]. Since magnetic hys-
teresis is path dependent, an identical instantaneous B(t)
value may correspond to different H(t) values depending
on the preceding state and recent trajectory. Time-domain
simulation is widely used in the converter design to evaluate
transient electrical behaviour and provide sizing information
for magnetic components before hardware implementation [5].
For voltage-driven magnetic components, B(t) is determined
by the applied excitation, whereas H(t) represents the cor-
responding material response and determines the magnetis-
ing behaviour. Therefore, precise converter-level simulation
requires magnetic models capable of reproducing the complete
time-resolved H(t) response.
Manufacturer datasheets typically provide permeability
characteristics under specified test conditions, while in prac-
tical engineering calculations, the permeability of magnetic
core is usually assumed to be constant. Although useful for
preliminary design, this approximation neglects the nonlinear
permeability and history-dependent local response of magnetic
materials, limiting its accuracy in reproducing PWM-induced
minor loops and saturation-dominated transient responses.
Physics-based hysteresis models, including the Preisach and
Jiles–Atherton formulations [6], [7], represent magnetic mem-
ory, but are fundamentally constrained by minor-loop assump-
tions and a low-dimensional state description, respectively.
Consequently, parameter calibration alone cannot reproduce
the frequency-dependent and partially traversed B–H tra-
jectories generated under arbitrary PWM excitation. These
limitations motivate a more complex data-driven transient
Modeling approach that can learn the nonlinear, multivariable
relationship directly from measured B–H trajectories and their
associated operating conditions.
Neural networks are universal function approximators and
are natural for learning high-dimensional and nonlinear mag-
netic relationships directly from experimental data, without

before estimating core loss [16]. MagLearn 1 formulated core-
loss prediction as a sequence-to-scalar (Seq2Sca) problem and
introduced a two-stage transfer-learning strategy comprising
pre-training on data-rich materials and fine-tuning on the
target material [17]. Despite their different formulations, these
approaches are designed for full-cycle modeling, where the
complete excitation waveform is available. In contrast, the
present task requires forecasting the unknown future H(t)
response from a partially observed magnetic trajectory. There-
fore, these formulations do not directly address the present
transient prediction problem.
The availability of transient measurements through Mag-
NetX database [18] and MagNet Challenge II extended data-
driven magnetic modeling to time-resolved prediction un-
der non-periodic PWM excitation. The MagNetX foundation
model adopts a sequence-to-scalar LSTM and constructs a
future trajectory autoregressively by returning each predicted
H value to the model memory [19]. While effective for
sequential generation, this autoregressive formulation may
accumulate prediction errors over longer forecast horizons.
RHINO-MAG [20] propagates a compact GRU hidden state
after an initial warm-up interval, with particular emphasis on
parameter efficiency, whereas LSTM-DAO [21] combines re-
current feature extraction with direction-dependent asymmetric
hysteresis operators. These studies establish the feasibility of
data-driven transient magnetic modeling, but some aspects
remain less systematically investigated, including quantitative
identification of the informative magnetic-history region, re-
duced robustness in saturation-dominated regions, and cross-
material adaptation under limited target-material data. These
considerations motivate several complementary design ques-
tions addressed by the MagLearn 2 framework developed in
this work, building on the successful MagLearn 1 concept [17].

The main contributions are summarised as follows:
• A condition-aware Seq2Seq formulation is developed
to predict the multi-step transient H(t) response from
the historical B–H trajectory and the known future
B(t) excitation, while allowing independent history and
forecast length. A lightweight saturation-aware auxiliary
module is introduced to selectively correct the saturation-
dominated predictions, while preserving the non-saturated
baseline response. This addresses a critical gap missing
in existing approaches, leading to high prediction error
in saturation regions.
• A performance-driven transfer-learning procedure selects
a compatible base model through target-specific valida-
tion and applies material-specific fine-tuning under lim-
ited target-material data. The mean and 95th-percentile
relative RMSEs average 3.75% and 8.75% across ten
base-model materials, and 4.69% and 10.83% across five
unseen materials after fine-tuning, with three of the five
target materials achieving top-three rankings in MagNet
Challenge II.
• The contribution of magnetic history to transient pre-
diction is characterised through history-length analysis
and the first application of occlusion-based sensitivity
analysis to data-driven transient B–H prediction. The
results reveal that the informative magnetic memory is
predominantly concentrated near the prediction bound-
ary, which is a missing insight in existing approaches.
This finding shows that history-dependent transient mag-
netic prediction does not necessarily require fundamental-
cycle-scale memory and provides a quantitative basis for
short-window modeling and history length selection.
II. MOTIVATION FOR DATA-DRIVEN TRANSIENT
MAGNETIC MODELING
Magnetic permeability (µ), as one of the key parameters
of ferromagnetic materials, characterizes the nonlinear re-
lationship between magnetic flux density B and magnetic
field strength H. Magnetic component manufacturers typically
specify three types of permeability in their datasheets: initial
permeability (µi), reversible permeability (µrev), and ampli-
tude permeability (µa). These parameters are normally used
in static modeling for either small-signal circuits or idealized
closed B–H loops.
However, in practical power-electronics applications, the
excitation is large-signal, and the B–H trajectory contains
nested, unclosed minor loops due to the coexistence of the fun-
damental and switching harmonics. In such cases, as discussed
in [19], the incremental permeability (∆µ) is more suitable
to capture the time-varying B–H dynamics. Over a discrete
interval [tk−1, tk], the incremental permeability is defined as

This dispersion arises because the incremental permeability
depends not only on the instantaneous magnitude of B, but
also on the preceding B–H trajectory, including the direction
of traversal and recent reversals. Under PWM excitation, each
switching sub-cycle traces a minor loop, while the aggregation
of these minor loops forms the overall major quasi-static loop
[26]. The same instantaneous B(t) value may therefore occur
on different branches of the major loop or along different
minor-loop trajectories, reflecting different preceding magneti-
sation histories and resulting in different local H(t) responses..
The incremental permeability is further affected by operating
conditions such as temperature, excitation frequency, and DC
bias.
For comparison with conventional material specifications,
the extracted incremental-permeability values ∆µ, are super-
imposed on the manufacturer-provided amplitude-permeability
characteristic in Fig. 1b. The datasheet curve provides a
single amplitude-permeability value, µa =

each peak flux-density amplitude ˆB under specified periodic
test conditions. It is useful for material characterisation but
is not intended to represent the instantaneous local slope
of an evolving B–H trajectory under PWM excitation. Al-
though ∆µ and µa are not quantitatively equivalent, the
comparison highlights the limitation of representing transient
magnetic behaviour using a fixed permeability formula or a
static single-valued B–H mapping. Such approaches cannot
capture the continuously evolving minor loops and history-
dependent responses encountered under non-periodic PWM
excitation, thereby motivating a sequence-dependent modeling
framework.
A data-driven neural-network model is a suitable method for
addressing these limitations. It can learn the complex nonlinear
relationship between B and H directly from measured B–
H trajectories, thereby reducing reliance on a fully spec-
ified analytical description of the underlying magnetisation
mechanisms [10], [27]. This approach is particularly suitable
for magnetic-material modeling because neural networks can
capture the multidimensional dependencies associated with
waveform shape, frequency, temperature, and magnetic history.
However, a conventional feedforward neural network does
not inherently retain information from preceding time steps
[28]. Since the transient H(t) response depends on both the
instantaneous excitation and the preceding magnetic history, a
recurrent architecture is required to preserve temporal memory
and capture the history-dependent hysteresis dynamics.
Among recurrent architectures, we select LSTM for its
gated memory mechanism, which retains relevant historical
information over long sequences while suppressing less in-
formative past observations [29]. The Seq2Seq framework is
adopted because it can map a historical input sequence to a
future output sequence while allowing the history and forecast
windows to have different lengths. Therefore, a condition-
aware LSTM-based Seq2Seq encoder–decoder architecture is
developed, as described in the following subsection.
III. SEQ2SEQ FRAMEWORK AND MODELING PIPELINE
The objective of transient B–H modelling considered in
this work is to reconstruct the unknown future H(t) response
from the available magnetic history and the known future B(t)
excitation. We adopt an LSTM-based sequence-to-sequence
encoder-decoder strategy to reconstruct the corresponding
H(t) sequence from the magnetic history and known B(t)
trajectory, with temperature and effective PWM frequency
included as conditional variables. The architecture allows the
history and forecast windows to have different lengths. The
following sections describe the model architecture and con-
figurations, training and inference procedures, and saturation-
aware auxiliary module.
A. Model Architecture
1) LSTM-Based Seq2Seq Encoder-Decoder Structure:
LSTM is a recurrent neural network specifically designed
to learn temporal dependencies in sequential data, making it


4
Fig. 2: LSTM-based Seq2Seq encoder–decoder architecture with operating-condition embedding for transient H prediction.
particularly suitable for modeling transient processes where
the current state depends on previous observations. Compared
with conventional recurrent neural networks (RNNs), LSTM
introduces gated memory cells that alleviate the vanishing-
gradient problem and enable relevant historical information to
be retained over long sequences [22]. Building on this capabil-
ity, a sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) formulation is adopted
since transient magnetic modeling requires mapping a histor-
ical magnetic trajectory to a future response sequence rather
than predicting a single scalar quantity. In this framework,
the encoder transforms the historical sequence into a latent
representation of the magnetic history, while the decoder uses
this representation to generate the future response sequence,
which is suitable for capturing the history-dependent dynamics
and reconstructing the time-resolved magnetic response [30].
As illustrated in Fig. 2, the proposed model constructs a
condition-aware LSTM-based Seq2Seq encoder–decoder ar-
chitecture. At each historical time step, the encoder receives
the paired flux-density and magnetic-field inputs [Bt, Ht]. It
processes their temporal evolution recursively and compresses
the historical B–H trajectory into the final hidden state hth
and cell state cth. The hidden state represents the extracted
historical features, while the cell state preserves the longer-
term memory accumulated over the historical sequence. Both
states are transferred to the decoder to initialise future se-
quence generation. Since the encoder and decoder process the
input and output sequences separately, the two sequences are
not required to have the same length [23]. This allows the
model to accommodate different history and forecast windows
without modifying the underlying architecture.
In parallel, the operating conditions, including temperature
and effective PWM frequency, are transformed into a latent
condition vector Zc through a condition-embedding module.
The historical B–H trajectories and conditional information
are processed through separate branches because they rep-
resent different forms of information. The LSTM encoder
extracts history-dependent magnetic features from sequential
waveforms, whereas the condition-embedding network maps
sequence-level operating variables into a compact latent repre-
sentation. This separation avoids repeatedly introducing static
operating variables into the encoder and allows the temporal
and conditional features to be learned independently before
decoding.
During the forecast interval, the future flux-density excita-
tion Bk is known and supplied to the decoder, consistent with
the voltage-driven operating conditions in most modern power
converters, where B is derived from applied voltage and H
is the unknown material response. At each forecast time step,
the decoder combines the future excitation with the condition
representation. Starting from the encoded state, it recurrently
updates its hidden and cell states and produces the predicted
magnetic-field value ˆHk. Instead of predicting a single scalar
quantity, the decoder reconstructs the complete time-resolved
future H waveform.

2) Full-Window and Short-Window Configuration:
a) Full-Window Configuration (Baseline):
The full-
window configuration is established as the reference setting.
As illustrated in Fig. 3a, each fundamental-cycle sequence is
downsampled to a fixed length and divided at the history
boundary th into a historical segment and a forecast seg-
ment. The paired historical flux-density and magnetic-field
sequences, B(t)his and H(t)his, provide the available magnetic
memory. The subsequent flux-density sequence, B(t)fcst, rep-
resents the known future excitation, while H(t)fcst is the cor-
responding response to be reconstructed. The length-adaptive
encoder–decoder architecture described in Section III-A1 can
process history and forecast windows of arbitrary lengths and
generates an H(t)fcst sequence whose length matches that of
the supplied B(t)fcst.
This full-window baseline achieves a mean relative error of
approximately 5.62% across ten materials. However, varying
the history-to-forecast ratio reveals that a longer historical seg-
ment does not necessarily lead to higher prediction accuracy.
In some cases, reducing the history length decreases the rel-
ative error, indicating that distant historical observations may
provide redundant information for the local transient response.
Furthermore, treating each complete sequence as a single
training sample limits the utilisation of local subsequences
within the fundamental cycle. These observations motivate
the short-window configuration introduced in the following
subsection, which restricts the historical context and extracts
multiple local samples from each full-length sequence.

2) Data Pre-Processing, Training, and Inference Workflow:
The overall workflow of data pre-processing, model training,
validation and inference is illustrated in Fig. 4. The original
sequences are first divided at the waveform level into mutually
exclusive training, validation, and testing subsets using a
60:20:20 ratio. This split is performed before subsequent
processing to ensure that samples derived from the same
measured trajectory cannot appear in different subsets, thereby
preventing information leakage between model development
and final evaluation. The training subset is used for model
optimisation, while the validation subset is evaluated at each
epoch without updating the model parameters. The testing
subset is applied only for the final performance evaluation.
To accommodate the variable-length raw sequences while
preserving their original temporal resolution, a fixed 1000-
sample window is progressively shifted along each original
sequence to extract local B–H trajectory segments. Each
extracted window is further constructed using three history-to-
forecast configurations, namely 1:9, 5:5, and 9:1, representing
different levels of magnetic history and prediction horizons.
This configuration is relevant to practical transient model-
ing, where the available historical information and required
forecast duration may vary with the operating scenario. It
also allows the same length-adaptive Seq2Seq architecture to
learn transient mappings over different prediction horizons
rather than being restricted to a fixed history-to-forecast split.
Sliding-window extraction simultaneously serves as data aug-
mentation by generating multiple local samples from each
measured trajectory and improving the utilisation of informa-
tion distributed throughout the original sequence.
The extracted segments and conditional inputs are subse-
quently normalised using scaling parameters derived exclu-
sively from the training subset, with the same transformations
applied to the validation and testing subsets. During each
epoch in the training stage, the same Seq2Seq model is
sequentially optimised using samples from the three history-
to-forecast configurations. For each batch, the predicted H
waveform is compared with the corresponding target wave-
form using a 5% quantile-trimmed mean-squared-error (MSE)
loss, and the model parameters are updated through back-
propagation using the Adam optimiser. The pointwise squared
errors within each batch are combined, and values above
the 95th percentile are excluded from the loss calculation.
This trimming reduces the influence of rare high-error points
(often associated with saturation events or under-represented
patterns) while maintaining optimisation over the dominant
inlier regime. Table III summarises the default hyperparam-
eters used in the majority of training processes. At the end
of each epoch, validation is performed independently for all
three history configurations without gradient updates. The
validation losses are then averaged with equal weighting. A
model checkpoint, which stores the trained network weights
and associated training state, is saved during training, and
the checkpoint achieving the lowest combined validation loss
is retained as the best model. During inference, each testing
sample undergoes the same preprocessing before being passed
to the trained model. The predicted H sequences are finally
transformed back to their original physical scale and compared

and Lint is the number of sampled time steps within the
observation interval. This descriptor characterises the density
of switching activity in the corresponding sequence. It is not
intended to recover the nominal switching frequency exactly;
instead, it provides a consistent representation of the temporal
switching characteristics that can be learned jointly with the
magnetic response.
The observation interval used to construct the frequency
descriptor depends on the amount of available history. When
the historical interval contains sufficient switching informa-
tion, the descriptor is evaluated directly from the historical
B segment. However, if the available history is too short to
provide sufficient switching information, it can instead be eval-
uated from the complete available B sequence, enabling the
conditional information to remain meaningful in extreme cases
where little or no H-history is available. Importantly, the same
descriptor definition must be maintained during both training
and inference, since the model learns the correspondence
between the conditional input and the associated waveform
dynamics rather than relying on its absolute numerical value
alone.

C. Saturation-aware Auxiliary Module
Magnetic saturation represents a distinct nonlinear operating
regime of ferromagnetic materials. Experimental characterisa-

saturation. For example, the relative permeability of a 3F3
ferrite decreases from a maximum of approximately 3900 to
around 200 as the magnetic field increases towards saturation
[32]. The pronounced changes motivate explicit consideration
of saturation in transient modeling [33]. Consistent with this
material behaviour, evaluation of the baseline Seq2Seq model
reveals that some of the most severe prediction failures oc-
cur when the forecast trajectory enters saturation region. In
these regimes, the baseline model tends to produce an overly
smooth H response and substantially underestimate the rapidly
increasing field strength.

IV. FEW-SHOT ADAPTATION TO UNSEEN MATERIALS VIA
FINE-TUNING
A. Representative Material-specific Industrial Scenarios
To evaluate the generalizability of the LSTM-based Seq2Seq
encoder-decode framework beyond the materials used for base-
model development, five unseen target materials are introduced
to represent different practical adaptation scenario. Rather
than differing only in material identity, these datasets reflect
limitations that may arise in engineering application, includ-
ing restricted labelled data, incomplete operating-condition
coverage, limited magnetic history, and material-level domain
shift. The benchmark therefore provides a representative test
of whether a model trained on data-rich reference materials
can be efficiently adapted to new materials under incomplete
measurement conditions.
Among the five materials, 3C92 is the ‘tiny data’ challenge,
with only approximately 15% of the training data available
compared to other datasets. This reflects practical situations
in which extensive waveform acquisition is expensive or im-
practical. 3C95 and FEC014 represent incomplete operating-
condition coverage, with missing switching-frequency and
flux-density regions, respectively. These cases require the
model to generalise to operating points that are sparsely rep-
resented or absent during target-material adaptation. FEC007
represents an extreme limited history case, in which only a
single historical H sample is available as an initial state before
the future waveform must be reconstructed. Such a condition
may arise when the model is initialized within a system-level
simulation. This scenario provides a particularly challenging
test because the lack of historical context effectively extends
the prediction horizon and increases susceptibility to error
accumulation with minimal prior magnetic-state information.
T37 represents the cross-material out-of-distribution general-
isation scenario, where the target material exhibits magnetic
characteristics differ significantly from those presented during
base-model development.
The five-target materials probe complementary aspects of
practical model transfer, ranging from data scarcity and
operating-domain mismatch to missing historical information
and material-level distribution shift. Training a separate model
from scratch for each case would require sufficient target-
specific data and repeated optimisation, which is impracti-
cal under several of these scenarios. Instead, the knowledge
captured by the data-rich base models is reused as an initial
representation and adapted to each target material through
transfer learning and few-shot fine-tuning. The corresponding
adaptation strategy is described in the following subsection.
B. Few-Shot Adaptation Procedure
1) Base-Model Selection: Transfer learning requires an ap-
propriate source model whose learned magnetic representation
is sufficiently compatible with the target material. Rather than
selecting a base model according to material characteristic
similarity, all ten models developed during the base-model
construction stage are systematically evaluated as candidate
initialisations for each unseen target material.
For each candidate, the corresponding pre-trained model
(in checkpoint file) is loaded into the same Seq2Seq archi-
tecture and using a short adaptation run (e.g., 100 epochs)
based only the training subset of the target material to avoid
information leakage. Validation losses from all history-to-
forecast configurations are averaged with equal weighting to
form a combined selection criterion. The model achieving
the lowest combined validation loss is retained for each
candidate base model. The candidate with the best combined
validation performance is then selected as the source model
for subsequent material-specific fine-tuning. This data-driven
selection strategy avoids relying on an assumed similarity
between source and target materials and instead identifies the
base model whose learned representation provides the most
compatible initialisation across the different history-to-forecast
conditions of the target material.
2) Material-Specific Adaptation Strategies and Fine-Tuning
Setup: Fine-tuning is performed end-to-end from the se-
lected base-model checkpoint, with all network layers re-
maining trainable. To preserve the transferable representa-
tion learned during base-model construction while allowing
material-specific adaptation, Adam is used with a reduced
learning rate, typically 1 × 10−4 to 3 × 10−4, and validation
performance is monitored to mitigate overfitting.
Material-specific preprocessing is then introduced according
to the characteristics of each target dataset. For the data-scarce
3C92 case, stronger augmentation is applied to increase the
effective amount of supervision. In addition to the original
waveforms, available historical segments with 500 and 900
samples are interpolated to 1000 samples and incorporated as
additional training trajectories, while a smaller sliding stride of
100 samples is used to extract a denser set of local windows.
FEC007 requires a dedicated treatment because only a single
historical H sample is available. The conventional history-to-
forecast ratio replication is therefore removed and the history
length is fixed to one sample, corresponding to an extreme
condition in which only the initial state is available. Since such
a short historical interval cannot provide a meaningful estimate
of the switching characteristics, the effective PWM-frequency
descriptor is calculated from the complete B sequence rather
than from the historical segment alone. A ratio-matched base
model is used before fine-tuning to reduce the discrepancy
between the source training configuration and this extreme

V. MODEL-SIZE OPTIMISATION AND HISTORY-LENGTH
ANALYSIS
A. Model Size-Accuracy Trade-off
The predictive capability of an LSTM-based model is
strongly influenced by its representational capacity, which
is primarily determined by the hidden-state dimension and
network depth. Increasing these parameters can improve the
ability to learn complex temporal dependencies, but simultane-
ously increases the number of trainable parameters, memory
requirements and computational cost. Previous studies com-
pared LSTM architectures with different parameter counts
and configurations, demonstrating that recurrent architectures
should be evaluated not only by predictive performance but
also by how effectively the available model parameters are
utilised [35]. Accordingly, the present study performs a con-
trolled architecture-size analysis to identify a suitable balance
between accuracy and model complexity for transient B-H
modeling.
A series of Seq2Seq models with different hidden sizes
and numbers of LSTM layers are independently trained using
the same N87 dataset and training framework, with all other
settings held constant. The investigated configurations range
from H64-L1 to H192-L3, where H and L denote the hidden-
state dimension and the number of LSTM layers, respectively.
Table V summarises the resulting model sizes in terms of
trainable parameter count, parameter memory, and stored
checkpoint size. For each architecture, convergence is assessed
using the combined training and validation behaviour across
the different history-to-forecast configurations.
Fig. 7 compares the mean and 95th-percentile sequence-
level relative errors obtained with different model configu-
rations. Increasing the model capacity initially produces a
substantial improvement in prediction accuracy, with H128-L3
Fig. 7: Prediction accuracy of different LSTM model configurations.
achieving corresponding errors of 3.07% and 6.60%. However,
the improvement becomes marginal relative to the increase in
model size. Increasing the hidden-state dimension from 128
to 160 raises the trainable parameter count from 860,929 to
1,342,401, an increase of approximately 56%, while reducing
the mean relative error by only 0.06 percentage points and
the 95th-percentile error from 6.60% to 6.28%. The H192-
L3 configuration contains more than twice as many trainable
parameters as H128-L3, while the corresponding prediction
errors increase slightly to 3.09% and 6.87%. It also exhibits
stronger transient fluctuations in both the training and vali-
dation losses before eventual convergence, suggesting greater
optimisation instability with excessive model capacity.
Although H160-L3 model configuration achieves the lowest
absolute prediction errors, its improvement over H128-L3 is
small relative to the substantial increase in parameter count
and memory requirement. H128-L3 therefore represents a
favourable size-accuracy operating point and is selected as the
reference architecture for subsequent training processes. These
results demonstrate that increasing model capacity beyond the
required complexity for the current task may provide diminish-
ing accuracy returns and introduce unnecessary computational
and memory overhead without corresponding improvements
in generalisation.
B. Model Interpretability: History-Length Sensitivity and Con-

Although

leading to the degradation of generalisation [36], [37]. There-
fore, the complete history from the point of prediction to the

effect of history length on predictive performance, followed by
an occlusion-based sensitivity analysis to identify the regions
that contribute most strongly to the prediction.
Fig. 8: Illustration of progressive history occlusion for sensitivity analysis.
Fig. 8 illustrates the process of the occlusion-based sen-
sitivity study. Occlusion sensitivity is a perturbation-based
interpretation method in which selected input regions are
masked and their importance is assessed from resulting change
in the model performance [38]. Masking a subset of the input
can be interpreted as replacing the selected components with a
fixed value, thereby making the corresponding historical infor-
mation unavailable to the model [39]. This principle is adapted
to the trained Seq2Seq LSTM to quantify the contribution
of different historical segments without retraining a separate
model for each history length [40]. Since the prediction target
is the future H waveform, the analysis focuses on the H-
history to quantify the model’s sensitivity to the preceding
magnetic response. As the synchronously sampled B and
H sequences jointly describe the same magnetic trajectory,
occluding either historical channel would follow the same
general principle of removing historical trajectory information.
For each sample, the unmasked history (m = 0) is first used
to obtain the reference prediction and baseline relative error.
The oldest m points of the H-history are then replaced by a
fixed masking value, while the remaining history, the complete
Bsequence and operating conditions are kept unchanged. The
masked sample is passed through the model again, and its
sequence-level relative error is calculated against the true
future H waveform. This procedure is repeated by progres-
sively increasing m until the entire H-history is masked. The
resulting error variation is used to evaluate the effect of the
available history length on prediction accuracy, as supported
by previous studies relating LSTM prediction error to the
number of preceding time steps [41].
Fig. 9 shows representative occlusion-sensitivity results for
different ferrite materials, total history lengths and operating
conditions, including different switching frequencies and tem-
peratures. The analysed samples are randomly selected from
the original sequences and cover different history-to-prediction
length ratios (e.g. 1:9, 5:5, 9:1). At each masking level,
the sequence-level relative error is calculated as the RMSE
between the predicted and measured future H waveforms,
normalised by the RMS value of the measured waveform. The
blue solid curve represents the mean relative error across all
selected samples at each remaining unmasked history length,
while the dark- and light-blue shaded regions indicate the
25th–75th and 5th–95th percentile ranges, respectively. As the
available history is progressively reduced, the prediction error
generally increases gradually at first, followed by a rapid rise
when only the most recent history is preserved.
To identify the point at which the prediction error begins
to increase rapidly, the error-growth characteristics are further
evaluated. The green dashed curve represents the mean error-
growth rate, obtained from the first derivative of the relative-
error curve for each sample, indicating the rate at which
prediction error increases as historical information is removed.
The green solid curve represents the corresponding mean
error-growth acceleration, obtained from the second deriva-
tive, which characterises how rapidly the error-growth rate
increases. The error-growth threshold, denoted by the orange
vertical line, is defined at the maximum positive error-growth
acceleration, indicating the onset of rapid deterioration in pre-
diction accuracy. The enlarged views highlight this transition
region and the identified threshold for each representative case.
Fig. 10 further illustrates how the required H-history can
be quantified for a prescribed prediction-error level, using
T37 with a 500-sample history as a representative example.
The horizontal lines indicate selected sequence-level relative-
error levels. Their intersections with the mean relative-error
curve determine the corresponding remaining unmasked H-
history lengths, as presented by the vertical dashed lines. In
this example, relative errors of 20% and 30% correspond
to approximately 357 and 281 remaining history time steps,
equivalent to 34.92 µs and 27.62 µs, respectively. From an
engineering perspective, this relationship enables the history
window to be selected according to a prescribed accuracy
requirement: once an allowable prediction-error limit is spec-
ified for a particular application, the corresponding H-history
provides an estimate of the historical information that should
be retained to satisfy that requirement. This establishes a
practical trade-off between prediction accuracy and history-
window length, allowing unnecessary historical inputs to be
removed and thereby reducing the computational and memory
requirements during model deployment.

proximately 800 kHz, and the highest temperature of 75 ◦C.
Accurate reconstruction is maintained under this demanding
combination of operating conditions. For FEC007, the close
agreement between the measured and predicted H responses
in Fig. 11c and 11g demonstrates that the model remains
capable of reconstructing the subsequent magnetic dynamics
under severely restricted historical information. Finally, Fig.
11d and 11h illustrate a strongly saturated 3C90 case using
the saturation-aware auxiliary module. Although H increases
sharply to several hundred A/m in the saturation region,
the proposed framework successfully captures both the rapid
waveform variation and the strongly nonlinear B–H trajectory.
Collectively, these representative cases demonstrate robust pre-
dictive fidelity across a broad range of challenging operating
scenarios, including variations in material characteristics, ex-
treme frequency and temperature conditions, severely limited
historical information, and saturation behaviour.
Compared with next-step transient magnetic modeling ap-
proaches, the proposed Seq2Seq framework jointly encodes
the paired historical B–H trajectory and directly reconstructs
the complete future H sequence from the known future B ex-
citation. This formulation avoids explicitly feeding previously
predicted H values back into subsequent forecast steps, as
adopted in single-step autoregressive approaches such as Mag-
NetX [19], thereby eliminating a recursive prediction-feedback
mechanism through which multi-step errors may accumulate.
This distinction is particularly relevant under severely limited-
history conditions such as that represented by FEC007, where
only minimal prior magnetic-state information is available.
In addition, sequence-level operating conditions are processed
through an independent condition pathway rather than being
repeatedly introduced into the recurrent history encoder. The
effective PWM-frequency descriptor further provides a com-
pact representation of local PWM cycling and the associated
minor-loops when the nominal switching frequency is not
directly available.
The proposed framework also differs from physics-inspired
hybrid approaches such as LSTM-DAO, which combines
recurrent networks with predefined hysteresis operators [21].
In contrast, the Seq2Seq structure learns the transient input-
response relationship directly from paired magnetic trajecto-
ries, while specialised mechanisms are introduced only where
additional robustness is required. A dedicated auxiliary mod-
ule for selectively correcting saturation-dominated predictions


15
has not been reported in existing transient B–H modeling
studies. The proposed module provides targeted correction in
saturation regions while preserving the non-saturated baseline
prediction. Combined with the few-shot adaptation strategy,
the pretrained model can be transferred from data-rich source
materials to unseen target materials with limited labelled mea-
surements. The framework therefore integrates direct future-
sequence forecasting, condition awareness, saturation-specific
correction, and cross-material adaptation within a unified
architecture.
VII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented an LSTM-based Seq2Seq encoder-
decoder framework for time-resolved transient B–H mod-
eling under PWM excitation. The framework reconstructs
the complete future H waveform from the historical B–H
trajectory and known future B excitation, with operating con-
ditions to further increase prediction accuracy. A lightweight
saturation-aware auxiliary module selectively improves robust-
ness in saturation-dominated regions. Transfer learning with
performance-driven base-model selection enables adaptation to
unseen materials with limited data. Occlusion-based sensitivity
analysis further shows that the most informative history is the
recent local B–H trajectory over only a few PWM cycles.
History dependence therefore does not necessarily require
fundamental-cycle-scale memory, providing both a physical
interpretation and an engineering basis for selecting the re-
tained history length.
The framework is evaluated across 15 magnetic materials,
achieving an overall mean relative RMSE of 4.06% and a
material-averaged 95th-percentile relative RMSE of 9.44%.
The deployment and evaluation code for MagLearn 2 is pub-
licly available on GitHub [44]. The resulting waveform-level
material model can potentially be integrated into converter
simulation software to reproduce transient B–H behaviour
under realistic PWM excitation. Such integration could support
more accurate component sizing and optimisation, reducing re-
liance on conservative design margins and thereby facilitating
higher power density and more efficient magnetic components.
